## Find the decision {#section-0}

“Use AI to improve student life” is an ambition. “Can students find the next club meeting without opening three different pages?” is a question we can investigate. Smaller questions make room for observation.

Start with a moment when someone needs to decide what to do. What are they looking for? Which details are missing? Write the situation down before choosing a tool. A shared description helps the group notice when it is solving different problems.

## Make something people can try {#section-1}

Sketch the information that person needs. A title, a short explanation, and a clear next action may be enough for a first test. Ask a classmate to use the sketch while you watch. Their hesitation can reveal something that a polished presentation hides.

Navigation labels are part of that experiment. “Perspective Lab” sounds interesting, but does not tell a newcomer what they will do. “Check its answers” gives a more concrete cue. The words next to a link can help people decide whether it is worth following.

## Leave room for a better idea {#section-2}

Write down what surprised you, including the parts that worked without explanation. Choose one change, try it, and observe again. Small iterations give the next person something specific to respond to.

At a student club, a paragraph, a sketch, or a working demo can start the same kind of conversation. An unfinished idea is useful when the question is clear enough for someone else to join in.
